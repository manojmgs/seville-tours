## 1. The problem, stated properly

> **Loyalty in tourism is structurally impossible for a single operator, because the customer leaves the city and does not come back.**

Every loyalty programme that works — Starbucks, a supermarket, a gym, an airline — depends on one thing: **the customer returns to the same place.**

A Seville tour operator has the opposite situation:

- The guest was here for **two days**
- They go home to Lyon
- They will next be in Spain in **three years**
- And it will be **Granada**

Carlos can give that guest a 10% discount card. It is worthless. Not because the guest didn't love the tour — they did, they left five stars — but because **the thing he can offer can never be redeemed.**

So the operator does the only rational thing: nothing. No loyalty. No reward. No return. The best guest relationship in the business evaporates on the train home.

That is the problem. And it is not a marketing problem — **it is a structural one, and it cannot be fixed by one operator, ever.**

---

## 2. Why operators cannot solve it themselves

Running a loyalty programme requires six things a micro-business does not have:

| Requirement | Why Carlos doesn't have it |
|---|---|
| Identity across visits | No account, no list, no CRM |
| A ledger of earned value | Would need accounting for issued liability |
| Memory across years | The relationship lives in a WhatsApp thread he'll scroll past |
| A reason to return | He is in **one city**. The guest is going somewhere else |
| A way to honour rewards | He can only honour his own product, on his own dates |
| Liability handling | Expiry, balances, refunds, tax treatment |

Over **70% of experience operators are micro-businesses.** None of them will build this. None of them should have to.

**This is infrastructure. It has to be shared.**

---

## 3. The four things that make tourism loyalty actually work

Fix the structure, not the marketing. Earned value has to be portable in four directions:

| Portable across… | Meaning | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Cities** | Earn with Carlos in Seville, redeem with Áloratur in Granada | The guest is going somewhere else — follow them |
| **Time** | Years, not a 90-day expiry | Travel cycles are years long. A 90-day reward is an insult |
| **People** | Give it to someone else | They may not come back. Their sister might |
| **Form** | Redeem as an experience **or as an object** | This is the one everybody misses — see below |

### The fourth one is the breakthrough

Here is what kills every tourism reward scheme: **most earned value is never redeemed, because the guest never returns.** It expires. It becomes a broken promise and an accounting embarrassment.

Unless it can turn into something that comes to *them*.

> **A keepsake is the redemption valve that makes tourism loyalty possible at all.**

The guest who will never return to Seville can still redeem:

- the photo Marco took of them at the Alcázar
- a printed book of the journey they actually had
- a ceramic tile in the azulejo pattern they stopped at, shipped to Lyon
- a bottle of oil from the grove they drove past

**Dead value becomes a delivered object.** The operator honours the reward. The guest feels remembered. The value doesn't rot in a ledger.

That is not retail. **That is the mechanism that makes the whole loop close.**

## 4. Four subjects of loyalty — travel rewards only one

Everybody builds guest loyalty. Nobody builds the other three, and the other three are where the leverage is.

### 4.1 The traveller

Standard, but done honestly.

### 4.2 The guide — **nobody does this**

Guests remember **Marco**, not "Seville Tours Co." They say his name in reviews. They ask for him by name a year later.

And Marco gets: nothing. No record, no portable reputation, no share of the memory he personally created. Guides move between operators — which is exactly why a guide-level record spreads the network faster than any operator-level one.

**A contribution record that belongs to the guide and travels with them** is a reward nobody in this industry offers, and it costs us almost nothing to give.

### 4.3 The referring guest

*"My friend went with you last year."* Today that is untracked, unrewarded, invisible. It is also **the highest-converting demand in the business**, and it is free.

### 4.4 The referring operator

Carlos sends work to Granada. Granada sends nothing back for eight months. Carlos stops sending.

**Bókun tracks commission. Nobody tracks balance of favour.** A two-sided, non-monetary count — *you introduced 4, received 1* — makes an informal network self-policing, is trivial to build, and is structurally uninteresting to a commerce platform.

## 5. This is not a new rail — it *is* scenario B

The most important structural point in this document:

> **The operator-handoff rail and the loyalty rail are the same rail.**

Earning in Seville and redeeming in Granada requires exactly what a context handoff requires: a record of who introduced whom, a consented identity, an attribution trail, and a way for one operator to recognise a traveller another operator sent.

Build the handoff and you have built network loyalty. Build network loyalty and you have built the handoff. **One thing, two pitches** — and the second pitch is far more interesting to an operator than "better enquiry forms."

## 6. Mechanics — and a hard no to points

**No points. Ever.**

Points create a second currency, an accounting liability, expiry disputes, tier anxiety, and behavioural-pressure mechanics that belong to airlines. *1,850 Para Usted points · Silver Traveller* is a product nobody asked for.

Instead — **bounded, named, operator-funded rewards**:

| Type | Example | Cost to operator | Who funds |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Recognition** | *"Returning Andalucía traveller"* · *"Marco's guest"* · *"You were here in 2024"* | **Zero** | Nobody |
| **Object** | The guide's photo · photo pack · journey book · route print · local keepsake | Low | Operator or traveller |
| **Value** | *"€15 toward another eligible experience"* | Real | Issuing operator |
| **Access** | Priority on a private date · a stop not on the public tour · the guide they asked for | **Zero** | Operator's discretion |
| **Gift** | Earned value handed to someone else | Neutral | — |

**Recognition and access cost nothing and are the most powerful two.** An operator in Granada being told *"this traveller came through Carlos in Seville in 2024, and asked for a historian"* is worth more than a discount — to both of them.

### Trip Plan grammar applies to rewards too

`Earned → issued → delivered → viewed → redemption started → honoured → expired`

Never say *"you have a free tour"* unless a date and a booking exist. Same discipline as `saved ≠ requested ≠ sent ≠ confirmed`.

## 7. Seven innovations — the presentable part

### 1. Earned in one city, honoured in another
Carlos issues. Áloratur honours. Carlos gets attribution and reciprocity credit. **No single operator can do this. A network can.**

### 2. The redemption valve
When a return trip won't happen, earned value becomes a delivered object instead of expiring. **Loyalty that survives never coming back.**

### 3. Recognition that outlives the operator
The next operator knows who they're dealing with — *returning traveller, came via Carlos, historian, relaxed pace, needs step-free access* — with the traveller's consent. **Better service, not a discount.**

### 4. Guide-level loyalty
Guests follow people. Let the reputation belong to the person who earned it and travel with them.

### 5. Gift as the loyalty transfer — loyalty that *acquires*
Earned value handed to someone who is actually going. **A loyalty scheme that recruits new travellers instead of only retaining old ones.** Nobody does this.

### 6. Seasonal reward weighting — yield management for a one-person business
> **"Your €15 is worth €40 in February."**

Airlines and hotels shift demand with reward value. A solo operator never could. **August and December kill them — this is the instrument that fills those months**, and it costs the operator nothing when it isn't redeemed in peak.

### 7. The anniversary return
One year later: *"A year ago today you were standing here. Marco told you this."* Highest open rate in travel, zero acquisition cost, arriving exactly when next year's trip is being planned — and it carries a brief that already knows they liked historical cities and local food.

**Permission-based, revocable, and separate from marketing consent.** Non-negotiable.

## 8. The operator's shelf — print-on-demand, on their own page

**Every operator gets a shelf on their own slug.** Their souvenirs, their gifts, their merchandise, listed under their name, on their page.

### We do not curate by segment — the customer chooses

The question *"would a couple in their forties want a t-shirt?"* is the wrong question and we should stop asking it. **The operator lists what they want to list. The customer picks what they want to buy.** It self-selects. A luxury operator lists a print, a ceramic tile and a book. A walking-tour company lists a shirt and a tote. Neither of us has to guess.

### Print-on-demand is what makes it work

| Property | Consequence |
|---|---|
| No inventory | Operator risks nothing |
| No minimum order | Works at quantity one |
| Customer picks size / colour / variant | Standard POD options at checkout |
| Made after the order | No unsold stock in a back room |
| Ships anywhere | **The guest never has to come back, or carry it** |

### Branding is the operator's call

Three options, chosen per product by the operator:

- **Operator-branded** — their name, their mark
- **City-branded** — *Sevilla*, *Granada*, *Córdoba*, no operator mark at all
- **Unbranded** — the design alone: the route, the date, the pattern, the place

An operator running a luxury positioning can sell a city-branded print and never put their logo on cotton. An operator who wants their name everywhere can have it. **Not our decision.**

### Design ownership sits with the operator; we supply the tooling

**We facilitate. They own.** We provide hundreds of templates — route maps, date prints, monument line art, pattern tiles, city typography, photo layouts — and the operator selects, edits, approves and publishes. The published design is theirs. Same provenance discipline as the tour catalogue: sourced, dated, permissioned.

That also settles the IP question cleanly. We never put an operator's name on a product they did not approve.

### Bought before the trip, not only after

This is the part that gets missed. **People plan before they travel.** If the shelf is visible while the Trip Plan is being built, the souvenir becomes part of the journey instead of an afterthought — and it solves a real problem nobody solves: *you do not want to carry a ceramic tile around Andalucía for ten days.*

Three fulfilment choices, per order:

1. **Ship home** — it's waiting when they get back
2. **Waiting on arrival** — hotel, or the operator's meeting point
3. **Collect on the day** — from the operator or the maker

### Cross-operator ordering

A traveller holding value can order from **the operator they travelled with, or from any other participating operator's shelf.** That is what turns a shelf into a network, and it is the same permission and attribution rail as everything else in this document.

## 9. What happens to unused value — the waterfall

The guest bought €100 and is not going to use it. Five paths, in order of value preserved:

### 1. Use it
Best outcome. Nothing to design.

### 2. Give it
To someone who *is* going. Free customer acquisition for the operator — a new traveller arrives holding value they did not buy.

### 3. Convert it — **at full face value**
€100 becomes €100 of merchandise, from that operator's shelf or another's. The value is never marked down.

> **Conversion beats resale for everyone: €100 of something is worth more than €80 of cash.** If this step works well, most people never reach step 4 — and that is the design intent, not a shortfall.

### 4. List it for resale, at the holder's own price

€100 listed at €80. If it sells, we pay the seller. If it doesn't, nothing is lost — **the voucher stays theirs.**

**I was wrong to call this discount leakage.** The operator was already paid €100, at full price, at the point of sale. The €80 changes hands between two travellers and never touches operator revenue. Whoever walks through the door is a customer the operator did not have and did not pay to acquire.

It is not a discount on the primary product. **It is liquidity on a secondary one** — and it makes the primary voucher easier to sell, because the buyer's biggest objection (*"what if it goes unused?"*) disappears.

The one residual risk is travellers learning to wait for resale rather than buying at full price. That needs deep, reliable liquidity to happen at all, which is years away — and it is a good problem to have.

Two operational controls, neither of which is a product decision:

- **A holding period before a voucher can be listed**, longer than the card chargeback window. This removes the buy-with-stolen-card, resell-for-clean-money pattern almost entirely — and it lines up naturally with when people actually realise they aren't going to travel.
- **Marketplace payments run through a licensed PSP**, seller as a connected account. We facilitate; we do not become a money transmitter.

### 5. The email, before expiry

> *"We couldn't find a buyer. Here's what you can still do with your €100 — use it, give it to someone who's going, or turn it into something we'll send you."*

At small scale there will be no buyers, and **that is fine — the listing attempt is what earns us the right to send this message and have it read as helpful rather than as marketing.** Resale creates the conversion moment even when resale itself fails.

### What about operator buyback?

I proposed this and **it doesn't work — because if the voucher expires, the operator keeps 100% for free.** Nobody pays €80 for something that becomes entirely theirs by doing nothing.

Buyback only becomes rational if **vouchers don't expire**, because then the liability never leaves their books and clearing it at 80% is real value. Worth revisiting once the Spanish expiry rules are confirmed with an adviser — several EU regimes restrict short expiry periods, which would make this live.

### The breakage decision, made on purpose

Unredeemed value is a large slice of profit in every normal gift-card business. A waterfall where value **always** finds a use deliberately destroys our own breakage revenue.

We should take that trade knowingly. *"Our vouchers never expire into nothing"* is an enormous trust claim and it is consistent with everything else in this product — but it must be a decision, not something discovered later when someone asks where the margin went.

## 10. What this looks like in A, B and C

**A — Carlos alone.** Issues his own recognition and objects. Anniversary trigger. Guide contribution. One completed experience produces a review, a keepsake sale, or a gift — with no CRM campaign.

**B — Operators collaborating.** The real unlock. Cross-city earn-and-honour, reciprocity between operators, recognition that travels, referral attribution that survives the handoff. **Same rail as the context handoff.**

**C — Multi-city operator.** One journey, one recognition record across three cities, rewards that don't fragment per supplier. Same hard stops: no inclusive price, no combined point of sale.

## 11. Where the money is — and why it's counter-seasonal

| Rail | Who pays | Difficulty | Season |
|---|---|---|---|
| **POD merchandise on the operator's shelf** | Traveller | **Lowest** — no inventory, no minimum, no capacity, no package exposure | All year, peaks Dec |
| **Pre-trip souvenir orders** (shipped home or waiting on arrival) | Traveller | Low | Follows booking season |
| **Keepsakes & memory objects** (redemption valve) | Traveller | **Low** — no capacity, no availability, no package exposure | All year, peaks Dec |
| **Operator gift cards** | Traveller | **Low — live today** | Peaks Dec |
| **Printed journey book** | Traveller | Low-medium | All year |
| **Ship-home** | Traveller | Low | All year |
| **Qualified introduction** | Operator | Medium | Follows demand |
| **City shelf sponsorship** (DMO / hotel / tourist board) | Sponsor, **labelled paid** | Medium | Annual budgets |
| **Resale listing fee** | Seller | Medium — needs a PSP and a holding period | Follows volume |
| **Network reward value** | Issuing operator | **High** — liability | Peaks Dec |

Two things fall out:

1. **The easiest rails have no incumbent and no regulator.** A keepsake is *goods*, not a travel service — no capacity, no double-booking, no Package Travel Directive exposure, retail margin instead of commission on €50.
2. **Every line is counter-seasonal to tours.** Tours peak in summer; rewards and gifts peak in December. **That is the reason an operator listens.**

We had this rail ranked last. It should sit immediately after the Journey Brief.

**Present it as a problem, not a product:**

> *"Your best guest leaves and never comes back. You can't run loyalty on your own because you're in one city and they're going to another. Nobody has solved that — and you can't solve it alone, by definition."*

Then three lines:

> **"Earn it with you in Seville. Honoured in Granada. You still get the credit."**

> **"If they never come back, it becomes something we send them — so it isn't a promise that quietly expires."**

> **"August and December kill you. That's exactly when rewards and gifts redeem."**

For a guide:

> **"They remember your name. Right now that's worth nothing to you."**

**What we must find out at TIS — past tense, phone-answerable:**

1. What happens after an experience ends?
2. How do you recognise a returning traveller? Show me.
3. Has a guest ever come back, or booked you in another city?
4. Has a guest ever said *"my friend came with you"*? How did you find out?
5. Have you ever offered a returning-guest discount? What happened?
6. **Would you honour a reward another operator issued?** ← the load-bearing question
7. Would you fund one for a guest going to another city?
8. Do you sell gift cards? Which type sells? What happens when the value is lower than the experience? Higher?
9. Who should hold the voucher liability?
10. **Which post-trip task would you pay to stop doing by hand?**

> *"Think about the last guest who referred someone or came back. How did you recognise the relationship, and what happened next?"*

## 13. Cautions — short, and at the bottom where they belong

- **Goods ≠ travel services.** A keepsake is retail. A keepsake *bundled with a transfer at an inclusive price* is a package. Keep them separate and this whole rail stays clean.
- **Never hold value we haven't designed for.** Operator gift cards work because ParaUsted holds issuance, balance and settlement. Network-wide reward value would put that on us — that is a company, not a feature.
- **Resale needs a holding period and a licensed PSP.** Not optional, and both are settled engineering rather than product questions.
- **Paid placement is labelled paid.** A sponsored city shelf is fine. A sponsored shelf disguised as taste ends us.
- **Third parties in photos.** A guide's photo of guests can contain people who never consented. Solve that before shipping the photo product.
- **Never publish a design the operator has not approved.** Templates are ours; the published product is theirs.

**Settled, and no longer open questions:** what to list (the operator decides), whether to brand it (operator, city or neither — their call), who owns the design (the operator), and whether the segment wants it (the customer chooses).
